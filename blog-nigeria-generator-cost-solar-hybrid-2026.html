Generator power above $0.40 per kWh: how Nigerian businesses should run the numbers

October 18, 2026 · 6 min read · By SunVolt Energy

Generator power in Nigeria is reported at more than USD 0.40 per kWh. That figure, published in October 2026, is the number that decides whether a solar-hybrid system pays back — and it is higher than most owners assume when they compare a generator and a battery system on purchase price alone.

This guide shows how to build the same number for your own site, so the comparison uses your costs rather than an industry average.

Why cost per kWh matters more than the generator price

A 5 kVA generator is cheap to buy and expensive to run. The purchase price is a one-off; fuel, servicing, downtime and replacement are continuous. When diesel prices move, the running cost moves with them, and a business that budgeted on last year's price is now paying a different number per kWh.

Reporting through October 2026 covered both sides of this: generator power above USD 0.40 per kWh for factories turning to solar-hybrid plants, and a diesel price surge putting network operations under pressure. Either way, the operating cost is what decides the investment case.

Build your own cost per kWh

Four inputs are enough:

  1. Fuel consumption per hour at the load you actually run, taken from the generator data sheet rather than the no-load figure.
  2. Fuel price per litre delivered to your site, not the pump price in another state.
  3. Running hours per day, including warm-up and low-load running.
  4. Maintenance reserve: oil, filters, servicing, and a replacement allowance over the generator's life.

Cost per kWh = (fuel per hour × fuel price + maintenance per hour) ÷ average load in kW. Worked example: a generator running at 3 kW average, using 1.4 litres per hour, with fuel at NGN 1,500 per litre, costs NGN 2,100 per hour in fuel — NGN 700 per kWh before maintenance. Substitute your own delivered fuel price and metered load; the method is what matters.

What a solar-hybrid system actually changes

  • Daytime load is served by PV, so generator hours drop.
  • The battery covers the evening block and carries critical loads through outages.
  • The generator stays for heavy or continuous loads, but runs far fewer hours.

The saving is rarely "the generator disappears". It is "the generator runs two hours instead of ten". That is the honest pitch, and it is the one that still looks right after the first month of operation.

Size it the right way round

  1. List the loads that must run, with watts and hours per day.
  2. Separate daytime loads that PV can serve directly from night loads that need the battery.
  3. Size the PV array for daytime energy plus battery charging, using local peak-sun hours.
  4. Size the battery for the night block plus a reserve, and check usable kWh and cycle life.
  5. Size the inverter for the simultaneous peak, not the total daily energy.

What to ask a supplier

  • Cell chemistry and cycle life, with warranty terms in writing.
  • Inverter surge rating, not only continuous rating, for motor loads.
  • Whether the system accepts grid, PV and generator inputs together.
  • Spare parts and after-sales support in your market.
